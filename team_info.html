United We Move Forward: The Fight to Unionize Starts With Us

June, 2026

Every day, warehouse workers keep this operation moving. Selectors, forklift drivers, loaders, sanitation crews, cooler teams, freezer teams, dry warehouse workers, and everyone in between all play a critical role in making sure customers receive their orders on time. We work hard. We meet demanding production standards. We adapt to changing expectations. Yet when decisions are made about our jobs, our pay, and our future, the people doing the work are often the last to be consulted. That doesn't have to remain the reality. The effort to form a union isn't about creating conflict. It's about creating balance. It's about giving every warehouse employee a meaningful voice in the decisions that directly impact our lives, our families, and our future.

"NO TIME GIVEN"

How many times have we heard the words, "No time given"? On the surface, it sounds like a routine management decision. But for employees whose pay is based on performance—measured by time allotted versus time completed—those three words can have a direct impact on our earnings. When a selector is delayed because inventory hasn't been replenished, when a forklift is blocking an aisle, when equipment breaks down, when product is missing, when pallets are wrapped incorrectly, or when another department's delay prevents us from doing our job, those lost minutes aren't our fault. Yet too often, the answer is simply, "No time given." The question every warehouse employee should ask is: Why? If management recognizes that an issue occurred, why is there no consistent process for compensating employees for time lost due to circumstances completely outside their control? Without clear standards, the decision becomes subjective. One employee may receive indirect time while another in the exact same situation is told no. That creates inconsistency, frustration, and uncertainty—especially in a workplace where incentive pay depends on production percentages. No one is asking to be paid for not working. We're asking for fairness. If the company expects us to be evaluated on performance, then performance should be measured based on the time we actually have available to perform—not the time lost because of operational problems we didn't create. This is exactly the kind of issue that belongs at the bargaining table. A union contract could establish clear, negotiated guidelines identifying which situations qualify for indirect time, how it is documented, and how it is applied consistently across every department and every shift. Instead of relying on individual discretion, employees and management could agree on standards that are fair, transparent, and applied equally to everyone. When our paycheck depends on production, fairness in measuring that production isn't a privilege—it's a necessity. Employees deserve to know that when unavoidable delays happen, they won't be penalized for problems beyond their control. That's not asking for special treatment. That's asking for accountability and a system everyone can trust.

Focus Points

Below is a shortlist of the intitial focuses and reasoning for pursuing a union.

  • Make sure we are fairly compensated, and all pay changes are negotiated instead of arbitrarly executed with complete disregard for the effect it has on our abilities to provide for ourselves and families
  • ensure managerial mistakes like understaffing different departments dont fall on workers causing us money with no recourse. This is includes the constant rotating from department to department, selectors losing time and money due to not enough forklift drivers, forklifts losing time due to bad pallet placement in the upper racks, etc. They expect productivity from us, we expect coordination from them.
  • No more arbitrary discipline, simply because they decided one issue is causing issues. They have a point system policy that they completely bypass solely based on what you did, discipline should be consistent
